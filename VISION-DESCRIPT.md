## 2. Incorporating Vision Models into the Workflow

Standard text editors break when there is no speech (e.g., long pauses, physical actions, B-roll). This is where vision models transform the application into an intelligent video tool.

### A. Scene Boundary & Visual Error Detection
Instead of slicing video blindly at word boundaries, you use vision models to analyze the aesthetic and physical structure of the shots (Selby-Lara, 2025).
*   **Models:** PySceneDetect (or TransNetV2) for hard cuts, and Vision-Language Models (VLMs) like Gemini Flash or Clip ViT for semantic scene grouping (Ding et al., 2025).
*   **Application:** You can automatically detect physical defects (camera shake, bad focus, low-light framing) or identify when an actor drops character/looks away between takes (Huh et al., 2023; Qi et al., 2025). In your UI, these regions are flagged directly over the script, letting users "delete dead space" or "remove blurry footage" instantly (Huh et al., 2023).

### B. Smart Multi-Cam Switching (Talking Head vs. Screen)
If your input has multiple visual elements (e.g., a webcam feed and a screen recording), vision models can automate the cutting logic.
*   **Method:** Run an object detection or face mesh model (like MediaPipe or a lightweight YOLO variant) over the webcam timeline. 
*   **Logic:** If the model detects the speaker looking away from the camera or gesturing broadly toward an object, the system can automatically cut the visual track to the screen capture while keeping the audio intact.

### C. Automated Intelligent B-Roll Insertion
When a user speaks about an abstract concept (e.g., *"We deployed a new database cluster"*), a text-only tool cannot contextualize it.
*   **Method:** Pass sentence-level script segments to a VLM or an embedding model (like SigLIP or CLIP) to extract semantic keywords.
*   **Execution:** Use these text embeddings to query a visual vector asset library (Qdrant/Faiss containing tagged stock footage or B-roll). The vision model aligns the chosen B-roll clip exactly over the duration of that spoken phrase (Huh et al., 2025).

## 3. Resolving the "Jump-Cut" Artifact (Generative Vision)

The biggest drawback to Descript-style text cutting is the **jump-cut**—the abrupt visual pop that happens when you splice two non-consecutive video segments together. Vision models solve this through temporal smoothing.

<Sequence>
{/* Reason: Procedural pipeline to fix the primary artifact of text-based video cutting. */}
  <Step subtitle="Frame Analysis" title="Identify the Cut Point">
    Locate the spliced boundary frames (Frame $A_{end}$ and Frame $B_{start}$) generated by the text deletion.
  </Step>
  <Step subtitle="Motion Vectors" title="Optical Flow Tracking">
    Run a motion estimation model (e.g., RAFT or UnFlow) between the frames to calculate the structural velocity and shift of the speaker's face and body.
  </Step>
  <Step subtitle="Frame Interpolation" title="Generative Inbetweening">
    Pass the boundary frames and their flow vectors into a video frame interpolation model (such as RIFE or Film). The model synthesizes 3 to 5 artificial "morph" frames that smoothly transition the speaker from position A to position B.
  </Step>
  <Step subtitle="Timeline Assembly" title="Render & Inject">
    Inject these synthesized frames into the rendering pipeline at the splice junction, turning a jarring jump-cut into an unnoticeable, fluid movement.
  </Step>
</Sequence>
